Evolving Bots for a 2D Counter-Strike with NEAT

11 min read
By Declan Porter

I wrote a small top-down Counter-Strike clone in C#, one player against one, and used NEAT to evolve neural networks that play it. The networks trained only against each other. After 250 generations the champion's Elo rating had gone from 1000 to 6938. Against a 160-line scripted bot it never trained against, the final champion lost all 100 benchmark games. This post covers how the setup works, what the numbers show, and why those two results don't contradict each other.

The game

CS Lite is a grid map, 9 by 10 cells of 50 world units each, with two blocks of wall near the middle and one spawn at each end. The simulation runs at 60 ticks per second. Each player has 100 health, and every bullet does 25 damage, so four hits kill. The gun fires at most once every 0.2 seconds with one degree of random spread. Players move at 150 units a second and turn at one full revolution per second. If nobody dies within 120 seconds the round is a draw.

The map is 180° rotationally symmetric. That matters for self-play: player 2 sees the world rotated into player 1's frame, and its chosen move gets rotated back before it is applied. One genome can then play either seat without learning two versions of every behaviour.

What the network sees and does

Each decision takes 30 inputs:

  • A bias, my health, my gun cooldown, my facing as a cos/sin pair, and my x/y position.
  • The enemy as seen from me: bearing (sin and cos), distance, whether I have line of sight, their health, how directly they are aiming at me, and their cooldown.
  • Bearing to the next waypoint on a breadth-first-search path toward the enemy, so the network doesn't have to solve path finding from scratch.
  • Eight wall-distance rays at 45° steps around my facing direction, each reaching 6 cells.
  • The nearest enemy bullet in my local frame: whether one exists, its position ahead and to the side, its distance, and whether it is heading for me.
  • Time left in the round.

There are 14 outputs, all sigmoids, split into three heads: 9 for movement (stand still plus 8 directions), 3 for turning (left, none, right) and 2 for firing (fire, hold). Each head takes its argmax, so the policy is deterministic. A decision is held for 6 ticks, a tenth of a second, before the network runs again.

NEAT setup

NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies, Stanley and Miikkulainen, 2002) evolves both the weights and the shape of a network. New connections and new hidden nodes come from mutation, and every structural change gets an innovation number so that two genomes with different histories can still be lined up for crossover. To keep new structure from being wiped out before its weights get tuned, the population is split into species by a distance measure, and each genome's fitness is shared with the rest of its species.

I wrote the NEAT implementation myself. The run in this post used these settings:

Instead of fixing the compatibility threshold, I let it adapt. Every generation it grows by 5% if there are more than 15 species and shrinks by 5% if there are fewer.

Self-play

There are no labels and no reward from outside, so fitness has to come from games between networks. Each generation builds a panel of 8 opponents. Half come from a hall of fame of past champions, mixing the last five with older ones picked at random, so the population can't forget how to beat strategies it has already seen. The other half are random members of the current population. Every genome plays the same 8 opponents on the same random seeds and alternates seats, so fitness is comparable across genomes.

A single game is scored like this:

score = 2 + 1.0 * hits_dealt - 0.25 * hits_taken
if loss: score += time_survived / 120s
if win:  score += 4

The baseline of 2 keeps an ordinary loss above zero, which matters because selection and fitness sharing both divide by fitness. The survival bonus applies only to losses. Dying late beats dying early, but a bot that hides until the timer runs out gets nothing for it and scores below one that fought and lost.

How I measured progress

Fitness alone tells you very little in self-play because the opponents keep getting better. I logged two other numbers every generation.

Elo. The top three genomes by fitness each play the 10 most recent champions, once from each seat, on fixed seeds. The one with the most points becomes the new champion. Its rating is the Elo at which its expected score against those 10 matches its actual score. I add one virtual draw so the rating stays finite after a clean sweep.

CSBot. CSBot is a scripted player. When it can see the enemy it turns toward them, fires once its aim is within 4°, strafes in a random direction that flips about every 1.2 seconds, and tries to hold a range of around 220 units. When it can't see them it walks a breadth-first-search path toward them. No genome ever trains against it. Each champion plays the same 100 games against it, 50 from each seat, as an outside check on whether self-play is producing good players or just players that beat each other.

The Elo curve goes up almost in a straight line, from 1000 to 6938. The CSBot curve barely moves. Over the first 50 generations champions won 7.8% of games against it on average. Over the last 50 they won 12.6%. The best single champion, at generation 201, won 56 of 100. The final one won none, landing 1.76 hits per game while taking 4. Its shots hit 8% of the time.

Self-play did teach the population something. At generation 0, 83% of self-play games were draws and the average game lasted 108 seconds, close to the 120 second limit: the networks mostly wandered around without finding each other. By generation 20 the draw rate was 12% and games lasted 23 seconds. Hits dealt per self-play game went from 0.49 to about 2.5. The networks learned to seek each other out and shoot. They didn't learn to do it as well as a few lines of hand-written aiming code.

Why the Elo number climbs anyway

Every rating is relative to the champions just before it. A new champion only needs to score a bit over 50% against the last 10 to be rated above their average, and their average is itself the result of the same process. Over the run, champions won between 40% and 80% of their games against recent champions. That's enough to push the number up by about 24 points a generation forever, whether or not anything is getting better in absolute terms. Elo in a closed population tells you each player beats the one before it. It says nothing about whether the cycle as a whole is going anywhere. CSBot is the only number in this run that does.

What NEAT did with its structure

The adaptive threshold dropped from 0.85 to about 0.3 within 20 generations and stayed there. The reason is in the distance formula. Excess and disjoint genes are divided by the size of the larger genome, and every genome here starts with 420 connections. One new link moves the distance by about 1/420. Species ended up separated almost entirely by weight differences, so speciation was mostly not doing its job of protecting new structure.

The champions agree. The final one had 9 hidden nodes and 456 enabled links, against 0 and 420 at the start. Across the run the champion's hidden node count bounced between 0 and 12. The network that played was close to a single layer of weights with a few extra nodes attached.

Why it loses to CSBot

  • Turning is coarse. The network picks turn left, none or right, held for 6 ticks at a full revolution per second, so each decision turns 36°. CSBot sets its turn rate in proportion to its aim error every tick and fires inside 4°. Could the network even line up a shot reliably? An 8% hit rate fits this.
  • Noisy selection. Each genome plays only 8 games, and the champion's CSBot win rate swings from 0% to 56% between neighbouring generations. Is the champion picked by luck as much as skill?
  • Self-play blind spots. If every network shares the same weaknesses, nothing in training punishes them. CSBot strafes and aims precisely; maybe nobody in the population does.
  • Fitness shaping. Hits dealt count four times as much as hits taken. Does that reward trading shots over not getting hit?

What's next

First, a fix for whichever problem above turns out to be the real one, most likely a finer or continuous turn output. After that I want to run NEAT, a plain evolution strategy and PPO on this same game and compare them at equal compute: environment steps or CPU hours on the x-axis instead of generations. Evolution strategies parallelise almost perfectly, and I'd like to see how far that gets compared to NEAT's topology search and to a gradient method, on a problem I understand down to the last line of code.
