Top Down Shooter: Training a PPO Bot

C++ · Python · pybind11 · PyTorch · PPO Project 23

I've been learning Reinforcement Learning (RL) and wanted a project to practice what I'd learnt. The game is a simple top-down shooter, and the goal is to train a policy that beats a hand-programmed bot. I honestly thought this would take a day. Oh how naïve I was. It turned out to be a hard problem, and below is the whole journey: what broke, what I tried, and what finally got it to a 90% win rate.

The game and the first network

I started in C++, and the game came first. That part was easy, I've made much harder games than this. I wrote the header file and had Claude write the implementation. Two players, an empty arena, shoot the other one until they die.

Then the network. I started with a simple 2-layer net:

  • Inputs: my position, health and rotation, the enemy's position, health and rotation, and the closest enemy bullet.
  • Outputs: four movement logits (left, right, up, down, each its own Bernoulli), a fire logit (Bernoulli), and a rotation output (a Gaussian mean with a hardcoded std of 0.2).
  • Rewards: a big reward for winning, a small reward for hitting the enemy, a small penalty for getting hit, and −0.001 per tick so it doesn't stall.

I trained it with my own PPO implementation in C++. To cut a long story short, it made almost no progress, even against a stationary opponent that never moved or fired. I didn't realise it at the time, but the amount of compute needed to get a good result here is huge. I wasn't sure what was going wrong, so I exposed the C++ environment to Python with pybind11 to iterate faster. I didn't write the bindings by hand either, that's boilerplate and AI is good at it.

Moving to Python

Python isn't my main language, so I started with a plain actor-critic. That failed too. The only reward for shooting arrives when the bullet hits, many ticks after the fire action. Without something like GAE to carry that reward back, the model can't learn that pressing fire was the right call.

So I implemented PPO, which turns out to be pretty easy in Python. Success, at least on the most basic task. In all the clips below the trained agent is blue and the bot is red.

Next I randomised the spawns to check it was really aiming and not just memorising one angle. I also added self-play and action repeats. Repeating each action for a few ticks means fewer policy queries, which speeds things up, and it lets rewards travel back further in time per decision. This worked too.

Training the real thing

Time to train against a bot that fights back. I bumped the network to 3 layers (one hidden layer of 128) and set action_repeats=2. I kept training with self-play instead of training against the bot directly, because self-play holds up better on hard problems, and used the bot purely as the benchmark. This run took 9 hours and ended at roughly a 30% win rate.

Giving it a memory

A single frame tells you where a bullet is, not where it's going. So I fed in the previous 4 frames as observations, which lets the model infer movement and dodge. I also spent time batching the environments to speed things up.

In the tables below, Greedy means the policy always takes its most likely action, and Stochastic means it samples from its distribution. The policy acts at 30Hz because of the action repeat. The bot runs at 60Hz, or 30Hz to match.

Dodging improved a lot. The bot's accuracy dropped from 11.0% to 6.9% and fights lasted about 30% longer. The policy was now more accurate than the bot (10.2% vs 6.9%) and landed more total hits (219 vs 194).

Making the hardware work

From here most of the work was engineering. I moved the threaded environment simulation into C++, since stepping it from Python was slow, and now C++ fills the buffers and just hands them to Python. Then a big batch size of 2048 so the tensors go to the GPU and it has something to chew on. On the model side I widened the hidden layer to 256 and gave it the 5 closest bullets instead of just one, to help it dodge.

The Bitter Lesson, again

This all comes back to Rich Sutton's Bitter Lesson. The only real changes to the model were:

  1. Adding a hidden layer.
  2. Putting more of the game into the inputs: the previous frames, and the 5 closest bullets instead of one.
  3. Widening the hidden layer to 256.

Everything else came from compute. All the effort went into making the CPU and GPU spin as hard as possible.

This opened my eyes to how much compute even a basic model like this needs. The final run would have taken over 60 hours on my original single-threaded implementation. On the GPU version it took about 40 minutes.
